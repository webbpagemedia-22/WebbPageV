US banks say customers who give AI agents their logins are on their own

Citi, JPMorgan, Bank of America and other big US banks have changed their terms so that customers who hand bank logins to AI tools carry the blame if anything goes wrong, even after a hack. It is a useful warning for anyone tempted to connect an AI assistant to the business account.

AI assistants are getting good at money jobs: sorting your spending, chasing bills and, in some cases, making payments. To do that, many of them ask for your online banking login. America's biggest banks have now made it clear who pays if that goes wrong.

Citi has updated its online banking agreement so that customers who share their login with a third-party AI tool are responsible for whatever happens through it. If the AI service is hacked and your details leak, anything the criminals do with your account is your problem, not the bank's. JPMorgan and Bank of America brought in similar rules last month, PNC has followed, and Citizens has emailed customers to say they are liable for what their AI assistant does.

Citi says it wants people to understand the risk of sharing banking details. Banks also have a commercial reason to be wary, as AI agents could steer customers away from fees and poor savings rates.

Not every tool works the same way. Anthropic's new finance feature for Claude, for example, is described as read-only and cannot move money.

The changes so far are in the US only, but the principle travels: once you hand your password to someone else, even a piece of software, it gets much harder to argue that a loss wasn't your fault.

What this means for your business

Don't give any AI tool your business banking login until you have read your own bank's terms on sharing security details. Where you do want AI help with your finances, prefer read-only connections through your bank or accounting software over handing over a password, and keep two-step login switched on.
