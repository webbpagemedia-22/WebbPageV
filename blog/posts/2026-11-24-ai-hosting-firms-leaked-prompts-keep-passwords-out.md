Many AI apps don't run their own models. Behind the scenes they pass your request to a hosting company, sometimes via a "router" service such as OpenRouter, which picks the cheapest or fastest one. New research shows how risky that chain can be.

Security firm Dragos spent July to November sending around two million test prompts to popular AI services. Each one contained a fake password or secret web link, so the researchers could see if anyone used it. They found that attackers had been inside three AI hosting providers for months, and none of the three knew until they were told. Some of the stolen prompts were marked "zero data retention", a setting that is supposed to mean they are never kept. Cut-price resellers on one marketplace were also simply logging what customers typed.

On 22 November OpenRouter said it had removed three providers, taken 125 endpoints off its zero-retention list as a precaution, and contacted affected customers. It says OpenRouter itself was not breached. The providers involved have not all been named.

The researchers' advice is to go direct to a major AI provider where you can, avoid bargain resellers, and never put real passwords or keys into a prompt.

## What this means for your business

If a tool you use relies on a little-known AI service in the background, ask the supplier which provider handles your data. Whatever you use, never paste passwords, bank details or customer records into an AI chat, and change any password you have already shared with one.
