AI phishing emails are a growing worry for small businesses in the UK. The Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) warns that criminals now use AI to write convincing, personal messages that pretend to come from colleagues, clients or suppliers you trust.

The old advice was to look for bad spelling. The National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC) notes that scams have become more sophisticated, and AI makes a polished email easy to write. So instead of checking the grammar, look at what the message wants. Does it claim authority, rush you, play on your feelings or tie itself to something topical, such as Christmas orders? Were you expecting it?

The costliest version is the fake invoice: an email that looks like it's from a regular supplier, saying their bank details have changed. Take Five to Stop Fraud, the UK Finance campaign, points out that firms rarely change their bank details, so any change deserves a challenge.

Give your staff this two-step check:

1. **Stop.** Never pay a new invoice, or change a supplier's bank details, on the strength of an email alone.
2. **Ring back.** Call the person on a number you already have, or one from their official website, never one in the email. For a first payment, send a small amount and confirm it arrived.

Forward suspicious emails to the NCSC at report@phishing.gov.uk. If money has gone, call your bank straight away. Turning on two-step login, as in our [Artex post](https://webbpageventura.co.uk/blog/2026-11-08-artex-ai-hacking-tool-exposed-switch-on-mfa), also makes your own email harder to hijack.

## What this means for your business

A well-written email is no longer proof that it's genuine. A two-minute phone call before any payment or bank change is the cheapest protection you have.
